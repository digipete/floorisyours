Understand Pete Hallett

Use this service to understand how a child from rural Dorset became a technology leader, founder, DJ, hairdresser owner, neurodiversity advocate and sponsored HYROX athlete.

You will need:
about 8 minutes, a tolerance for electronic music, and no assumption that any of this followed a sensible plan.

This service is in beta. Some personality features remain unexplained.

Step 1 of 8

Start in the Shire

I grew up around Bridport and West Bay in Dorset: countryside, fields, cliffs, sea and quite a lot of space. Technology was not exactly abundant. Being outside was.

The things I kept coming back to were technology and how things work, music, the coast and open air, and sport.

At the time they looked like unrelated interests. Much later I realised they had something in common: curiosity, intensity, freedom, pattern, movement and somewhere for my brain to go.

Step 2 of 8

Leave Dorset. Become the techie.

I moved away from the place I loved and somehow ended up in the thing that had been hardest to access there: technology.

That became software, consulting, government, startups and eventually companies operating internationally. There were teams, investment rounds, New York offices, Dubai, Lisbon, too many flights and a lot of learning in public.

Career progression is not the point of this service.

The useful bit was discovering that the problems I cared about were rarely just technical. They were usually people problems, process problems and systems problems wearing a technology hat.

I became a leader largely through trying to understand people: what helps them do good work, what gets in their way, why apparently sensible organisations behave strangely, and how to make difficult things easier to understand.

Step 3 of 8

Acquire several side quests

Technology has never been the whole story. A reliable feature of my life is becoming intensely interested in something and then finding a way to get involved properly.

SIDE QUEST

Quiet Space Club

For a long time I described this as being curious, restless, entrepreneurial or simply incapable of leaving things alone.

Then, in my 30s, I got a much better explanation.

Understand the neurodiversity bit

When I finally understood my neurodivergence, a lot of apparently random parts of my life made more sense. Not as a list of clinical symptoms, but as patterns I recognise in myself.

Pattern recognitionI often see connections, risks and routes through a problem before I can fully explain why.
Predictive listeningI am frequently several sentences ahead of a conversation and have to make a conscious effort to wait for reality to catch up.
People radarTiny shifts in tone, behaviour and dynamics can feel very loud to me. It has shaped how I lead and how quickly I form a view of a situation.
Novelty cliffI can learn a new puzzle very quickly. Once the puzzle is solved, my brain may immediately begin looking for another one.
Low tolerance for repetitionThis one requires very little additional documentation.

Retrospective finding:
The countryside, the ocean, music, building companies, difficult organisational problems and later sport were not random. They were all places where my brain found either stimulation, pattern or calm.

Step 5 of 8

Change something I thought was fixed

Warning

There may be some semi-nudity on this page.

Sport was not always part of my identity. For a long time I was much heavier, work and businesses took most of the oxygen, and fitness was something other people seemed to be good at.

Then I changed something I had assumed was simply part of who I was.

Transformation in progress
  • Lost about 9 stone
  • Started training seriously
  • Found CrossFit
  • Found HYROX
  • Started racing
  • Kept escalating

The important bit is not that I got fit. It is that I discovered I could completely change something about myself that I had treated as fixed. Sport gave me another system to understand: patterns, numbers, repetition, discomfort, progress and the chance to find out where the edge actually is.

HYROX is now a genuine part of my life in its own right. I race, I train, I travel to compete and I have become a sponsored athlete. It belongs here because it changed me — not because it makes a good photograph.

Step 6 of 8

Keep the bits that make life feel like life

Music has been there since school, when I was made to play the violin. Later it became production on an Atari ST, DJing, clubs, festivals and eventually making music again properly.

And, more importantly, music has always been about people. Some of my closest friendships were built around records, clubs, ridiculous nights out and the shared belief that going home was probably premature.

Then we lost James

In August 2025 one of my best friends died in a freak accident. James was painfully close to breaking through in the music scene. Losing him changed what making and playing music meant.

We now play B2B sets he should have been booked for, have remastered his unreleased music, and arranged for it to be released through an underground label.

The money from the sets and music goes into a trust created to support his son, Albert.

Music was already tied to friendship. After James, it became tied to memory too.

That is why this belongs in the life story rather than as a footnote about a hobby. It is about the people around the music, what happens when one of them is suddenly missing, and finding a way to keep some of what they were building moving forward.

Step 7 of 8

Turns out, life was happening too

Somewhere amongst the companies, technology, music, training and everything else, I also became a parent.

I still can't work out whether becoming a parent made me better at work, or whether becoming better at work made me a better parent.

Maybe both happened at the same time.

I've always treated my kids a bit like my mates. Apparently that's not necessarily recommended parenting practice, but it seems to work for us.

There are three fairly different brains in our house, and over time we've found our balance mostly by talking to each other, being pretty honest about how we're feeling, and working things out together.

I'm not sure that's very different from what I've eventually learned about building teams, either.

Listen properly. Assume people are different. Talk about what is actually going on. Give each other enough room to work things out.

Some things did not change.

The kid who wanted to understand how things worked, the founder, the technologist, the DJ, the athlete and the person you work with are largely driven by the same brain.

Patterns. Problems. People. Novelty. Intensity. And, every so often, getting back to the sea.
